# 15 — A hot finish to the season predicts nothing

**Confidence: High** (about a thousand player-seasons, and the answer is a firm, well-measured zero)

## TL;DR

"He finished the year on fire" tells you nothing about next season. A
player's last few weeks predict next year *worse* than his full
season does, and add nothing once you know his season average. The
kill shot: the last five weeks of a season are no more predictive
than the first five weeks of the same season.

## What we tested

For every offseason from 2017 through 2025, and every rosterable
player, we asked what predicts next season's scoring better: the full
season, or just the finish. About a thousand player-season pairs,
scored under our league's exact settings. We measured with
correlation, which is just a score for how well one number predicts
another — 0 means no connection, 1 means perfect.

## What we found

Full-season points per game predicts next season at roughly .5 to .6
depending on position. Every shorter window does worse: the last five
weeks, the last three, and the final game predict progressively less,
at every position. And once you already know a player's full-season
average, adding "how he finished" improves the forecast by
essentially nothing — the extra signal measures +0.04, which is
statistically indistinguishable from zero.

The cleanest test compared the last five weeks of a season against
the *first* five weeks of the same season. They predict next year
equally well. December games feel more meaningful than September
games; they aren't. A hot finish is a small sample wearing a story.

## The one flicker

Running backs are the lone spot where the tail end might carry a
whisper of extra signal — a back who takes over a backfield in
December sometimes really does own the job going forward. The
evidence is suggestive but didn't survive our stricter checks, so
treat it as a tiebreaker at most, and only when the *role* visibly
changed (the starter was traded or benched), not when a backup was
merely hot.

## Draft rule

Draft off full-season and multi-season rates. When an August ranking
or a family member cites "the way he closed the year," that
information is either already inside his season average or it's
noise. This pairs with
[touchdown luck regressing](#/blog/16-td-luck-regresses): recent,
vivid, story-shaped stats are exactly the ones to distrust.

## The 2025 scorecard

2024's 25 hottest finishers scored 0.8 points a game *less* in 2025;
the 13 coldest finishers lost only 0.3. Baker Mayfield closed 2024 on
fire and fell 20.7 → 15.1. Matthew Stafford limped to the finish and
jumped 12.5 → 19.8. That's what "the finish predicts nothing" looks
like when you bet it for a season — both streaks just washed out.
