# 13 — Handcuff running backs: real insurance, zero edge

**Confidence: Medium-High** (1,440 simulated seasons put the effect at almost exactly zero — a precise tie, not a shrug)

## TL;DR

Drafting your starting running back's backup — his "handcuff" —
really does pay out: about 9.5 points a game in exactly the weeks
your starter sits. But a drafter who pays extra to own his handcuffs
finishes in a dead tie with one who doesn't, because the insurance is
worth exactly what the picks it costs are worth. Take a handcuff on a
coin flip; never reach for one.

## The insurance is real

We found 124 starter-and-backup pairs over six seasons. When the
starter missed a week, the handcuff averaged 9.5 points a game —
right at low-end RB1 level — and hit that level in about half the
outage weeks. When the starter played, the same backup averaged 4.6,
below what the waiver wire offers. That's a nearly pure insurance
policy: worthless in good times, valuable in exactly the bad ones.
The 2025 case that prompted the question behaved to spec — Rachaad
White gave 7.7 points a game during Bucky Irving's eight missed
weeks, versus 3.6 the rest of the time.

## But it buys no wins

We put two identical computer drafters into our simulated league —
same behavior in every way, except one pays a premium from the middle
rounds onward to grab the backups of its own running backs. Across
1,440 simulated seasons they finished in a statistical dead heat:
.599 versus .598 all-play (all-play = your record if you had played
every team every week). That's not "we couldn't detect a difference."
The difference is measured tightly, and it's zero.

## Why it cancels out

A bench spot is a bet on the future, and the two bets pay the same.
The handcuff is a narrow bet: it pays only if *your* starter goes
down, and even then only the gap between him and your next-best
fill-in. The best-available player is a broad bet: he pays on any bye
week, any injury at his position, or just by outscoring your flex
some week. Narrow-but-big and broad-but-small come out even. And when
a starter does go down, the whole league can see the promoted backup
on waivers — owning him in advance is less exclusive than it feels.

## The rule

If your guy's backup and someone else's player are a genuine coin
flip in round 10, take yours — the insurance is free at that price,
and you'll set your lineup with less stress. Just never spend a round
early to get him. You're not buying an edge; you're buying a feeling.
