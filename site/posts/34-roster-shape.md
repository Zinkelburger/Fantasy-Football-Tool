# 34 — Roster shape doesn't matter, as long as you work the waiver wire

**Confidence: Medium-High** (eight roster templates against an identical field, same schedules and same random luck, 360 seasons each — with a second sweep that turns the waiver wire off and reverses the answer)

## TL;DR

Drafting to a plan — "one quarterback, three receivers, load up on backs", "balanced six and six", "always take a backup quarterback" — is worth nothing measurable compared to just taking the best available player as the board falls. Every template we tested landed within nine points of a season against a drafter with no rules at all. But that's only true because the waiver wire is quietly patching the holes a roster plan would have covered. Turn the wire off and shape suddenly matters a lot — and the most lopsided roster goes from best to worst.

## The sweep

We took the same value drafter, gave it a hard rule about how many of each position it was allowed to draft, and ran it against an identical league.

| Roster plan | Points vs no plan at all |
|---|---|
| Max backs, three receivers | −0.2 |
| Two quarterbacks | +0.1 |
| Eight backs, four receivers | −4.8 |
| Two tight ends | −4.6 |
| Five backs, seven receivers | −5.7 |
| Six and six, balanced | −8.6 |

The margin of error on each of those is about six points, so the honest reading is that the whole table is one flat line. The back-heavy end is very mildly the better end, which is directionally what you'd expect from running back scarcity — but no shape separates from the pack, and a value drafter ends up back-tilted in this league anyway without being told to.

## Why nothing matters — and what that says about the wire

Here's the puzzle. An empty starting slot is genuinely expensive: [our waiver research](#/blog/22-waiver-wire-reality) put it at 14.3 points when it's your quarterback. A full roster runs into about seven of those a season. So why doesn't drafting a backup quarterback as insurance pay off? It's worth **+0.1 points**.

Because the insurance is already free. The wire patches that hole for a waiver claim, and a claim costs you nothing but attention. A backup quarterback costs a draft pick that could have been a startable back or receiver. Both plug the same hole; only one of them charges you.

That's the same answer [finding 20](#/blog/20-second-qb-te) reached from a different direction and [finding 19](#/blog/19-bench-composition) from a third. Three independent tests, one mechanism.

## Take the wire away and it all reverses

Same templates, same luck, but the drafter is now forbidden from making any waiver claim:

| Roster plan | With the wire | Without it |
|---|---|---|
| Two quarterbacks | +0.1 | **−2.4** |
| Two tight ends | −4.6 | −7.8 |
| Six and six, balanced | −8.6 | −17.7 |
| Five backs, seven receivers | −5.7 | −20.1 |
| **Max backs, three receivers** | **−0.2** | **−21.5** |

The most lopsided roster is the best plan with a wire and the worst plan without one. Meanwhile the boring insurance plan — two quarterbacks — goes from pointless to the most robust thing on the board.

## What to actually do

**Don't draft to a template.** Take the value in front of you.

**But understand what a lopsided roster is: a promise to work the waiver wire.** If you're the manager who checks waivers twice a week, draft however you like — hoard backs, it costs you nothing. If you know you're a set-and-forget manager, stay balanced and take the backup quarterback. That pick is worthless to an active manager and the single most protective thing you can do if you aren't one.

**In PPR, stop hoarding backs** — the receiver-heavy shape moves from the worst end of the range to the best across the formats, though the effect stays small.
