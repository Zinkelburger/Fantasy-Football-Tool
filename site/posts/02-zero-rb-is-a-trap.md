# 02 — Zero RB is advice for a league we don't play in

**Confidence: High** (1,440 simulated seasons per strategy, and the
result got stronger when we imported the internet's best versions)

## TL;DR

Zero RB — deliberately skipping running backs early to load up on
receivers — is the most popular "smart" strategy in fantasy content,
and in our league it was the worst plan we tested: a 9.7% championship
rate versus 20.3% for opening with three straight running backs.
That's half your title odds, gone. The strategy was built for leagues
where catches score points. Ours isn't one.

## What we found

In the same simulation as [our RB-RB-RB finding](#/blog/01-robust-rb-wins),
the Zero RB team posted a .548 all-play — all-play meaning the record
you'd have if you played every team every week — against .598 for the
RB-first plan. A WR-heavy version failed the same way, just less
badly. Zero RB's one good season was 2022, the year the big running
backs all busted, which is exactly the year its thesis predicts. Even
then it only *tied* a team drafting with no strategy at all.

## Why it fails here

Zero RB needs at least one of three things to work: catches that score
points, a flex spot that takes tight ends, or a waiver wire with
startable running backs on it. Our league has none of them. Scoring is
standard, the flex is RB/WR only, and with twelve teams the other
eleven rosters hold about 66 running back spots — when a backup gets
promoted, he's claimed before you wake up. The receivers you hoarded
instead are the one thing the wire *does* keep producing.

## We tested Reddit's favorites too

A league-mate pointed us to a popular Reddit study that ranked draft
plans for PPR leagues (leagues that pay a point per catch). We rebuilt
its four named strategies and ran them in our format. Their #1 overall
plan lost to RB-RB-RB here, and the pattern across all four was
simple: the more early picks a plan spends away from running backs,
the worse it does in our league. Format isn't a detail — it's most of
the answer.

One honest limit: the simulator tests the *plan*, not talent. A
drafter with a genuinely great eye for receivers could beat these
numbers. But the plan itself starts you in a hole, and it needs an
RB-bust year just to climb back to even.
