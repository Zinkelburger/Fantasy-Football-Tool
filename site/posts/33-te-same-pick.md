# 33 — Draft a tight end early or very late. Never in between.

**Confidence: Medium-High** (the head-to-head holds in all three scoring formats and all eight drafts but rests on 26 early picks; the round-5-to-9 warning is stronger still, from 139 picks)

## TL;DR

The instinct that a receiver or running back is obviously a better use of an early pick than a tight end turns out to be wrong. From 2018 to 2025, tight ends drafted in the first four rounds returned more than the receivers and backs taken at the same picks 69% of the time, and they busted at half the rate. But the odds of getting a useful tight end fall off a cliff after round 4 — so take one of the two or three elite ones at market price, or wait until round 10 and take darts. The rounds in between charge a real pick for lottery odds.

## How you compare a tight end to a receiver

You need one currency. Ours is **points above the last starter at that player's own position** — the TE12 season for tight ends, the RB30 and WR30 seasons for backs and receivers in a 12-team league. The logic is simple: whichever position you skip at this pick, you'll fill later from what's left. So the gap between the tight end's number and the same-slot receiver's number is what your lineup actually gained or lost by choosing.

## Early tight ends win the trade — by not cratering

For each tight end drafted in rounds 1 to 4, we compared him to the average of every receiver and back drafted within six picks of him.

| | Standard | Half PPR | Full PPR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tight end beat his same-slot alternatives | 69% | 69% | 65% |
| Net season points per pick | +19 | +25 | +34 |
| Same, with Travis Kelce removed | +6 | +9 | +13 |

Kelce's seven seasons carry a big share of that, and it's only fair to say so. But even with him removed the early tight end was still the slightly better use of the pick, in every format. Never worse.

The reason isn't that elite tight ends boom more often. It's that they almost never collapse:

| Group | Boom | Bust |
|---|---|---|
| Tight ends, rounds 1–4 | 54% | **8%** |
| Receivers at the same picks | 46% | 24% |
| Backs at the same picks | 50% | 20% |

Comparable upside, a third of the downside. Eight drafts produced exactly two early-tight-end wipeouts against dozens of round-2 and round-3 zeroes at receiver and back. The market lets fifteen-plus receivers and backs go in the first four rounds and only two or three tight ends — and the ones it lets through that filter are the position's sure things.

## The cliff that rules out the middle rounds

| Drafted in | Finished top-6 | Finished outside the top 12 |
|---|---|---|
| Rounds 1–2 | 86% | 0% |
| Rounds 3–4 | 53% | 21% |
| Rounds 5–6 | 30% | 52% |
| Rounds 7–9 | 16% | 59% |
| Rounds 10–13 | 14% | 64% |

Past round 6, where a tight end is drafted tells you almost nothing — a round-7 tight end and a round-12 tight end are the same lottery ticket at different prices. That's what kills the middle rounds: you pay a round-5 price for round-12 odds, and walk out of the best part of the draft with a better-than-even chance that your only tight end isn't startable.

So: **the elite door** (one of the consensus top few, rounds 1–4), or **the wait door** (nothing until round 10, then two darts or a streaming plan). Not the hallway between them.

And do draft one. Living off the waiver wire at tight end is drying up — three, two and three top-8 tight ends went undrafted in 2020 through 2022, then zero, one and zero in 2023 through 2025.

## Scoring format moves the dial

Every tight-end edge above grows as receptions get more valuable, because elite tight ends are 90-catch players while the TE12 catches about 50. The overall edge goes from +19 points in standard to +34 in full PPR. In a PPR league, move the elite tight ends up about half a round to a round from where you'd take them in standard. In a standard league the elite door is at its least valuable — still real, but a luxury rather than a priority.

## Where we might be flattering the tight end

Two biases both push the same way, and they're worth stating. A dead tight-end pick only costs you the TE12 baseline (about 80 points), while a dead running back pick costs the RB30 baseline (about 110) — so busts hurt less on our scale at tight end. And the yardstick gives receivers and backs no credit for bench value: a WR35 season still covers a bye week, where a TE9 season gets cut.

Those two together are big enough to explain the modest middle-round numbers, which is part of why we don't trust the middle. They are not big enough to explain the first-four-rounds result or the cliff.
