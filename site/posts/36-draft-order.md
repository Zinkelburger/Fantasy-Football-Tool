# 36 — When you take a quarterback is the only draft-order decision that matters

**Confidence: High** (the quarterback timing and the two backup results are forced A/B tests over 1,200-plus paired seasons; the early-running-back count is observational and the weakest claim here)

## TL;DR

Most of what people worry about on draft night is worth nothing: when to take a tight end, whether to grab a backup quarterback, how to fill out the bench. One decision is worth 27 points a season. We forced each of those choices and measured it, and only one of them moved: **when you take your quarterback.** Take him by round 5 or 6, open with running backs, and stop worrying about the rest.

There is exactly one pick that's actively worse than doing nothing, and it's the backup tight end.

## Waiting on a quarterback costs you, every single round

We forced a drafter to take his quarterback in a fixed round and left everything else free — no rules about how many backs or receivers to take, so no version is ever pushed into a player it doesn't want.

| When the quarterback was taken | Points vs. an average team | all-play |
|---|---|---|
| Round 4 | **+10.1** | .611 |
| Round 6 | +2.0 | .604 |
| Round 8 | −9.7 | .592 |
| Round 10 | **−17.0** | .586 |

That's 27 points from the best version to the worst. Each of those numbers carries a margin of error of about 11 points, so no single row is proof on its own — but four versions landing in perfect order is the signal.

Notice what the table doesn't have: a cliff. There's no round where quarterback value falls off a shelf, so there's no moment to time. Every round you wait costs you a few points, which makes the rule simple: **once the early running back rounds are done, take the best quarterback there.**

**Streaming doesn't rescue you.** The usual answer to waiting is that you'll just pick up quarterbacks off the waiver wire each week. We measured that: a wire quarterback scores 11.4 points a week, a top-12 starter scores 19.1. Over a 14-week season that gap *is* the late-quarterback penalty. It isn't a plan, it's the bill ([finding 22](#/blog/22-waiver-wire-reality)).

**Getting clever doesn't help either.** We tried a conditional rule — "if I opened with a lot of backs, take the quarterback at 5, otherwise wait until 8." It scored +5.4, landing between the plain round-4 and round-6 rules and beating neither. Take the quarterback early no matter how the early rounds went.

## The backup quarterback does get used. He still wins you nothing.

This is the one that surprised us. Across 2,893 teams that drafted a second quarterback:

| | |
|---|---|
| Weeks he actually started | 3.3 of 14 |
| Never started at all | 17% |
| Started three or more weeks | 47% |
| **Took over as the main starter** | **18%** |
| His points per game in the weeks he started | 14.8 |

So "maybe one of them turns into something" is real. It happens about one year in five. And it still doesn't pay:

| | Points for | all-play | Made playoffs | Won the title |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Allowed a backup quarterback | 1191 | .602 | 76.9% | 21.8% |
| Banned from drafting one | 1192 | .601 | 78.0% | 21.4% |

Same teams, same schedules, same luck, 1,200-plus paired seasons: the championship difference is **+0.4%, give or take 2.9%.** Nothing.

He isn't useless — he's *redundant*. The weeks your backup starts are exactly the weeks you'd have grabbed someone off the wire anyway, and that wire quarterback scores 11.4 to his 14.8. You're buying three points a week, maybe six times a year, and paying a draft pick for it.

Draft one if you want him. Just don't build your draft around him.

## A backup tight end is worse than nothing

Banning the second tight end outright scored **+2.9** against +2.0 for keeping it. Real drafts agree: teams that took no late tight ends beat teams that took two, .531 to .507 in all-play, in every kind of roster build.

It's the same redundancy as the backup quarterback, but worse, because a tight end you never start has no trade value and no bye-week use. Spend the pick on anything else.

## Tight end timing barely registers

With the quarterback pinned at round 5 and only the tight end rule moving:

| Tight end taken | Points vs. an average team | Points from your tight end slot |
|---|---|---|
| Round 4 | +0.7 | 91 |
| Round 6 | −5.2 | 85 |
| Round 8 | −5.4 | 79 |
| Round 10 | −6.8 | 75 |
| Round 12 | −5.2 | 75 |
| Round 14, then stream | −4.4 | 73 |

Read those two columns against each other, because together they say something useful. Waiting really does cost you 18 points **in the tight end slot** — 91 down to 73. It costs your *team* nothing, because the pick you saved bought a better player somewhere else. That is exactly what a position you can live without looks like.

Every one of those rows sits inside its own margin of error, so take your tight end whenever. But see [finding 33](#/blog/33-te-same-pick), which asks a sharper question — what you gave up at that exact pick — and does find an answer: rounds 1 to 4, or round 10 and later, never the middle.

**Don't count on the wire here.** Tight ends who went undrafted and finished in the year's top 8 went 3, 2, 3 in 2020 through 2022, then 0, 1, 0 in 2023 through 2025 ([finding 05](#/blog/05-never-pay-up-for-te)). A hole at tight end patched off the waiver wire is worth 4.8 points.

## The bench doesn't care what you fill it with

Rounds 9 through 15, forced to one position, with the starting lineup already covered:

| The bench was | Points vs. no rule | all-play | Won the title |
|---|---|---|---|
| All running backs | +0.9 | .604 | 23.8% |
| All receivers | −0.7 | .602 | 22.8% |

Late backs minus late receivers: **+1.6 points, give or take 2.3.** Nothing.

This one overturns what ordinary drafts appear to show. Watching real drafts, teams that took four late running backs beat teams that took none by five points of all-play — a huge gap, and it holds no matter how they opened. But it's backwards. Those teams took four late backs *because* good backs kept falling to them, and falling backs are what the result is really measuring. Force the rule on everyone and the gap disappears. ([Finding 19](#/blog/19-bench-composition) found the same null a different way.)

The folklore that late running backs are the boom-or-bust pick is true, though (players going between picks 96 and 180, 2020–2025):

| Position | Put together a real hot streak | Was a dud | Points per game |
|---|---|---|---|
| Running back | **15%** | 42% | 4.5 |
| Receiver | 10% | 36% | 5.3 |
| **Tight end** | 23% | 20% | 4.9 |
| **Quarterback** | **31%** | **16%** | 13.9 |

A late back does catch fire more often than a late receiver. He also busts more often, and the two cancel out.

The real story in that table is the bottom two rows: **late quarterbacks and tight ends hit far more often than late backs and receivers.** That's why drafters naturally walk out with two quarterbacks (81% of them) and two tight ends (67%) — those late picks genuinely look better. It just doesn't win, for the redundancy reasons above.

## Open with running backs

This part is observational — 18,000 team-seasons of real drafts, nobody told what to take:

| Running backs in rounds 1–4 | Teams | all-play |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1,516 | .450 |
| 1 | 5,446 | .490 |
| 2 | 6,852 | .510 |
| **3** | **3,581** | **.517** |
| 4 | 605 | .506 |

Two or three, with three slightly ahead. A fourth starts to hurt.

Treat this as the softest claim on the page, for the same reason as the bench result: teams end up with three early backs partly because backs fell to them. But it points the same direction as three other things we've measured — 58% of *hindsight-perfect* drafts open with backs ([finding 24](#/blog/24-hindsight-optimal-drafts)), backs are worth more than receivers at the same pick ([finding 23](#/blog/23-early-rb-vs-wr)), and the strategy backtest agrees ([finding 01](#/blog/01-robust-rb-wins)). Four independent looks, one answer.

## What to actually do

1. **Rounds 1–4:** running backs. Take two or three. Don't force a fourth.
2. **Rounds 5–6:** the best quarterback available. Don't let it slide past 6.
3. **Tight end:** an elite one in rounds 1–4, or wait until round 10. Not the middle.
4. **Everything else:** receivers, wherever they fall.
5. **Rounds 9–15:** genuinely doesn't matter. No second tight end.
6. **During the season:** fill empty starting slots off the wire. Ignore the rest of it.

## Where this could be wrong

- **We used to get this wrong ourselves.** An earlier version of this work capped how many of each position a drafter could take, which forced it to reach past better players — worth about 10 points. Worse, those templates quietly pinned the roster to one quarterback and one tight end, so they were secretly testing "no backup" rather than what they claimed to test. Everything above constrains *when* you draft a position, never *how many*.
- One league room, built to imitate our family's drafters. A sharper room would change the quarterback result most, and in the direction of taking one even earlier ([finding 03](#/blog/03-adp-discipline-is-not-an-edge)).
- Injuries are the real historical record rather than reshuffled, so a player who missed eight games misses eight in every run. That means none of this sees the disaster year where your top three backs all go down at once.
- The early-running-back table is what happened in real drafts. Everything else here is a forced A/B test.
- Standard scoring, one quarterback and one tight end in the lineup. A superflex or tight-end-premium league would break both the quarterback and tight end results.
