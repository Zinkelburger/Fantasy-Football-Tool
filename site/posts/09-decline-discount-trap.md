# 09 — The bounce-back discount receiver is usually a trap

**Confidence: Medium** (only 17 players fit the profile, but the pattern held in every slice we cut)

## TL;DR

The "he was great two years ago, he'll bounce back" pick is usually a
trap. Mid-round wide receivers coming off a collapse year busted 53%
of the time — clearly worse than ordinary mid-round picks — and youth
didn't save them. A cheap price on a former star usually means the
market knows something, not that it missed something.

## Who we mean

Every draft board has a few receivers with the same résumé: a top-20
wide receiver two seasons ago, a collapse last season, and now a
friendly mid-round price. The pitch writes itself — "you're getting
the old version at a discount." We pulled every mid-round WR from the
last six drafts who fit that exact profile: 17 players, from DeAndre
Hopkins to Kenny Golladay.

## What we found

They busted 53% of the time, versus 42% for all other mid-round
receivers, and on average they finished about 24 ranking spots below
their draft price — nine spots worse than the ordinary mid-rounder.
Age didn't rescue anyone: the under-29 group busted 56% and the
29-and-over group 50%. And the misses weren't near-misses. They were
total zeroes — the Golladay and DJ Chark seasons you cut by October.

## Why the market gets this one right

A collapse usually has a cause that sticks around: fading speed, a
broken offense, a younger teammate who took the job. The discount
isn't the market missing something — it's the market knowing
something. The trap feels profitable because of selective memory:
everyone remembers Tee Higgins bouncing back, nobody remembers the
nine guys who didn't.

There's a cleaner rule hiding here. A mid-round discount is worth
buying when it comes from the player's *situation* — a good receiver
stuck on a bad team really does pay off
([finding 08](#/blog/08-bad-team-wr1-edge)) — and not worth buying
when it comes from the player's own play. It's also a different
question from plain age discounts, which turned out murkier than we
hoped ([finding 10](#/blog/10-wr-age-effects)).

## Where this could go wrong

Seventeen players is a small group, so the exact gap could shift as
seasons get added — that's why this is Medium confidence, not High.
And the few who did bounce back (Diggs, Keenan Allen, Higgins) share
a tell: they kept a big share of their team's targets even during the
down year. Volume surviving the collapse may be the real signal, and
it's the first thing we'll test when next season's data lands. We've
paid this tax ourselves, twice — see
[the audit of our own drafts](#/blog/11-methuen-jackels-audit).

## The 2025 scorecard

Three 2025 mid-rounders fit the fallen-star profile. Brandon Aiyuk
never made it back (no qualifying finish), Deebo Samuel middled
(drafted WR38, finished WR32) — and Stefon Diggs bounced back
(WR40 → WR24). Diggs is the escape clause in action: he kept 24% of
his team's targets through the down year. But honesty check — Aiyuk
kept 22% too, so the volume tell picked the hit *and* a miss. Two of
three fades right, and the tell stays a lean, not a shield.
