# 10 — The WR age cliff is real, but smaller than it looked

**Confidence: Medium-High** for "29 and older underperform their
price" — this one got *stronger* when we widened the test. Update
below.

> **Updated 6 August 2026.** This post tested age only in the middle
> rounds, and hedged to "use age as a tiebreaker, not a rule". We
> re-ran it over ten drafts at every price, always comparing a
> receiver to others who cost the same pick. The broad cut is the real
> one:
>
> | | Top-24 rate vs same-priced peers |
> |---|---|
> | **29 and older** | **−8 points** (range −15 to −1) |
> | 29–30 only | −3 points (range −13 to +9) |
>
> So it was never a two-year "death zone" — that framing was the
> overfit part. It's just age, and it shows up everywhere on the
> board. It survived every check we ran: three different definitions
> of a "hit", three ways of slicing price, both halves of the sample,
> and dropping any single season (the effect moves between −6 and −11
> points and never flips).
>
> Of our three receiver-archetype findings, this is the only one that
> came through the board-wide re-test — findings
> [08](#/blog/08-bad-team-wr1-edge) and
> [09](#/blog/09-decline-discount-trap) did not. It is also the only
> one the draft tool still states as a verdict.

## TL;DR

Our first pass found a tidy rule: mid-round receivers aged 29–30 are
a death zone, and 31-plus veterans bounce back. We stress-tested it
and only half survived. Older receivers really do underperform, with
29–30 the worst ages in most versions of the test — but the 31-plus
rebound was noise. Use age as a tiebreaker, not a rule.

## The version that was too good

Slicing six years of mid-round receivers by age gave a striking
picture: 29- and 30-year-olds hit (finished top-24) only 17% of the
time and busted 61%, while every other age group busted somewhere
between 33% and 45%. Better still, receivers 31 and older seemed to
bounce back. It came with a neat story — the market panics about age
at the wrong moment — and a ready-made draft rule. Which is exactly
when you should get suspicious.

## How we tried to break it

The danger with a result like this is called overfitting: slice 200
player-seasons into enough age buckets and one bucket will look
extreme by pure chance — and then your brain invents a story for it.
The cure is to change things that *shouldn't* matter and see whether
the pattern survives: split the years in half, widen and narrow the
draft-price range, move the definition of a bust.

The 29–30 slump passed most of those checks. It shows up in six of
seven versions of the test, in both halves of the data. But one
reasonable slice — looking only at the middle of the mid-round range —
erased it entirely. And the 31-plus rebound failed outright: it flips
direction depending on which years you look at, and the whole
"rebound" is a handful of specific players (Davante Adams twice,
Diggs, Keenan Allen, Hopkins) having good recent seasons.

## What we actually believe now

Receivers 29 and older underperforming as a group: probably real,
size uncertain. The 31-plus bounce-back: no claim should rest on it.
The practical version: between two otherwise-equal mid-round
receivers, lean younger — and let the sturdier filters make the real
decision. Buy discounts that come from a bad situation
([finding 08](#/blog/08-bad-team-wr1-edge)); don't buy discounts that
come from the player's own collapse
([finding 09](#/blog/09-decline-discount-trap)).

We're publishing the broken half on purpose. A site that only showed
findings that survived would quietly teach you that every tidy
pattern is trustworthy. Most aren't.

## The 2025 scorecard

Five receivers aged 29–30 went in 2025's mid rounds. Three busted —
Terry McLaurin (drafted WR15, finished WR50), Calvin Ridley (WR109),
Chris Godwin (WR79) — Deebo Samuel middled, and Courtland Sutton beat
his price (WR20 → WR12). Three clear busts in five keeps the caution
alive, and five players keeps the confidence capped where it is.
