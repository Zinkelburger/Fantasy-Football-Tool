# 18 — "Injury-prone" is mostly a myth

**Confidence: Medium** (the QB and WR result is solid; the tight end exception rests on only 24 repeat cases)

## TL;DR

Being hurt this year barely predicts being hurt next year. Across more than 1,200 player-seasons, injury history explained about half a percent of who misses time next season — and at quarterback and wide receiver the carryover is exactly zero. The exceptions are tight ends and, more weakly, running backs.

## What we measured

We counted the weeks each player was listed Out or Doubtful on the official NFL injury report from 2017 through 2025, then asked whether this year's missed time predicts next year's. For quarterbacks and receivers it doesn't — at all. Receivers who missed two or more weeks actually repeated slightly *less* often than the never-injured ones (23% vs 25%). That's what a true zero looks like: the noise falls on both sides.

Tight ends are the exception. A TE who missed two-plus weeks missed significant time again 38% of the time, versus 19% for healthy ones — double the rate. Running backs showed a weaker echo (30% vs 18%), shaky enough that we wouldn't build a draft rule on it. A plausible reason: the injuries that tend to recur — soft-tissue pulls, high-contact roles — concentrate at those positions.

We also checked whether injury history predicts *production*. It doesn't. Among players healthy enough to stay rosterable, missed weeks add nothing to predicting next year's points once you know this year's points.

## What to do with it on draft day

Never discount a QB or WR for injury history. When someone at the table calls a receiver "made of glass," that's a buying opportunity — the discount in his price is real and the extra risk isn't. A modest discount on tight ends coming off multi-week injuries is defensible; a small one at running back, maybe.

## The honest limits

Our data only covers players who made it back onto rosters. Someone whose injury ended his career, or wiped out his whole next season, has no next year to measure and drops out of the sample. So this finding is about the ordinary range — sprains, hamstrings, ankles — not Achilles tears. And "weeks listed Out" is a proxy that occasionally counts a player who was really benched, not hurt. Neither issue rescues the injury-prone label for QBs and receivers, but they're why we stop short of calling anyone risk-free.
