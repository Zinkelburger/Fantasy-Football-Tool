# 39 — Opportunity scores: what a player's touches are worth

**Confidence: High** (five seasons of play-by-play, 26,000 player-games, always scored on a held-out season)

## TL;DR

A player's opportunity score is what his carries, targets and pass attempts are normally worth, before anything he did with them. It is a better guide to next week than last week's box score at every position, and it is what the weekly QB/RB/WR/TE table on this site ranks by.

## What it is

Fantasy points are usage times luck. The usage half is sticky: a running back who gets 18 carries and 4 targets this week will probably get about that next week, because that is his job. The luck half — whether a goal-line carry becomes a touchdown, whether a deep target is caught — is not sticky at all. The opportunity score keeps the first half and throws away the second.

Every touch is priced by where on the field it happened and, for targets, how far downfield it was thrown. A carry from the 5-yard line is worth more than one from midfield; an end-zone target is worth more than a screen. Add up a player's touches at those prices and you have his expected points for the game. Average that over recent games, leaning on the newest, and you have his opportunity score.

## How well it works

On a season we never trained on, expected points explain about 55 to 65 percent of the game-to-game variance in actual points, depending on position. That is the ceiling for any usage-only number; the rest is the luck half. Over a full season the two lines come together: per-game expected points and per-game actual points correlate at .92 to .97 by position. The number is not a guess at talent. It is a description of the job.

The test that matters is prediction. For every player's second game onward in 2025, we asked which predicts his points better: a running average of his expected points, or a running average of his actual points. Expected points won at every position, by .02 to .04 in correlation. Small, consistent, free.

We also checked our prices against the ffverse expected-points model, a far more elaborate one that prices every play separately. The two agree at .95 to .98. The simpler model gives up almost nothing, and every price is a number you can read off the table in the full write-up.

## What to do with it

Start players by opportunity score first and matchup second. A wide receiver who has been scoring below his usage is the one to hold or buy; one scoring well above it is the one to sell while the touchdowns last. On the waiver wire, the pickup is the player whose expected points jumped before his actual points did — a new role shows up in the touches a week before it shows up in the box score.

The table updates Tuesday and Saturday mornings from the official play-by-play, with the week's Vegas team totals and the NFL injury report beside it.
