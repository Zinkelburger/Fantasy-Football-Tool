# 07 — How fast each position falls off a cliff

**Confidence: High** (plain averages over six full seasons)

## TL;DR

We averaged six seasons of points per game at every rank of every
position. Running back value falls off a cliff, receivers stay nearly
flat for twenty ranks, tight end is elite-or-nothing, quarterbacks
score the most but the last starter is still fine, and kickers are a
rounding error. Every draft rule on this site comes from this one
table.

## The table

Points per game by positional rank, 2020–2025, our league's scoring:

| Rank | QB | RB | WR | TE | K |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| #1 | 23.5 | 19.1 | 15.7 | 11.2 | 11.5 |
| #4 | 20.8 | 15.1 | 12.4 | 8.2 | 10.0 |
| #10 | 17.8 | 12.3 | 10.6 | 6.1 | 9.1 |
| #16 | 15.5 | 11.0 | 9.5 | 5.1 | 8.4 |
| #28 | 11.9 | 8.6 | 8.0 | 3.6 | 7.1 |

In our lineup — 1 QB, 2 RB, 2 WR, 1 TE, an RB/WR flex, and a kicker
across 12 teams — the "last starter" is roughly QB12, RB30, WR30,
TE12, and K12.

## How to read it

Running back is the cliff: the #1 back outscores the #10 by almost 7
points a game, and the position's entire premium lives in the first
dozen names. That's the engine behind
[opening RB-RB-RB](#/blog/01-robust-rb-wins).

Receivers are the opposite — the #7 wideout outscores the #28 by only
3.5 points a game. Mid receivers are nearly interchangeable, which is
[why WR-heavy drafting fails](#/blog/02-zero-rb-is-a-trap) and why the
mid-round WR game is about [picking the right ones](#/blog/08-bad-team-wr1-edge),
not picking them earlier.

Tight end drops from a genuine weapon at #1 to barely-above-the-worst
by #7, so [pay elite or pay nothing](#/blog/05-never-pay-up-for-te).
Quarterback has the biggest total drop, but the twelfth starter still
scores more than any RB2 — a high floor that makes
[waiting survivable](#/blog/06-qb-timing). And three points separate
the best kicker from the worst starter, the smallest gap on the board
([and you can't predict it anyway](#/blog/04-kickers-are-noise)).

## One caution

These are end-of-season ranks, which is hindsight — nobody knew in
August which back would be #1. A draft-day version of the chart is
noisier but the same shape. And averaging six seasons smooths real
year-to-year swings; the strategy backtests, not this chart, carry the
risk story.
