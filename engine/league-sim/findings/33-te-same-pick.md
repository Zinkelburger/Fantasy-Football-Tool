# 33 — Should you draft a TE at all? The same-pick test

**Confidence: Medium-High** for the head-to-head — consistent across
all three formats and 8 drafts, but 26 early picks concentrated in a
few players. **High** for the hit-rate cliff — n=139, and it matches
finding 29's independent cut.

Yes, draft one, through one of two doors.

"A WR or RB at that pick is obviously better than a TE" fails the
data. From 2018–2025, the TEs drafted in rounds 1–4 out-returned the
WR/RBs taken at the same picks 69% of the time and busted at **half**
the rate (8% vs 20–24%). The market only lets two or three
heavily-vetted TEs go early each year while early WR/RBs bust
constantly.

The hit odds fall off a cliff after round 4 (86% → 53% → 30% → 16%
top-6), so rounds 5–9 charge a real pick for round-12 odds. The whole
effect scales with receptions: weakest in our standard league,
strongest in full PPR.

Section 6 tested titles and narrowed the advice. Pre-committing to
*any* TE timing lost to simply taking the board: reaching for the
elite TE cost 3.7 points of title rate, paying his exact market price
cost ~1.7, and only taking him at a discount was free. So the rule is
not "buy the elite TE." It is **let him come to you: take him when the
board says he's the value, never pay a premium to guarantee you get
one, and never spend rounds 5–9 on the position.**

## The yardstick

To compare a TE season with a WR season you need one currency. Ours:
**season points over the year's last-starter season at the pick's own
position** — TE12 and QB12, RB30 and WR30 by season total (12 teams,
2RB/2WR/flex). Call it "points over the last starter." Whichever
position you *don't* take at this pick, you fill later from the
draft's leftovers, so the difference between the TE's number and the
same-slot WR/RB's number is the season points your lineup gained or
lost by the choice.

Lower is worse. In the charts below, a WR/RB group sitting below the
TE means those picks returned less — not that you should have taken
them.

A big chunk of "early TE pays" is the Kelce era. Remove him and the

same-slot WR/RB, in every format. Never worse. The 2025 rows preview
the post-Kelce version: Bowers at pick 18 lost to his slot by 51,
McBride at 26 beat his by 24, Kittle at 38 by 16.

## 2. Boom or bust — why the TE keeps winning the trade

Early TEs don't boom more. They almost never crater:

![Stacked shares of boom / in-between / bust for early TEs vs same-pick WR/RBs](../figures/te_boom_bust.png)

Standard scoring, early picks (rounds 1–4); boom = 25+ points over the
last-starter season, bust = 25 under:

| group | n | boom | in between | bust |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early TE, all | 26 | 54% | 38% | 8% |
| Early TE, no Kelce | 19 | 42% | 47% | 11% |
| Same-pick WR | 110 | 46% | 30% | 24% |
| Same-pick RB | 107 | 50% | 30% | 20% |

Comparable boom rates, one-half to one-third the bust rate. Eight
drafts produced exactly two early-TE wipeouts (Pitts 2022, Waller
2022) against dozens of round-2-3 WR/RB zeroes. The market drafts
15-plus WR/RBs in the first four rounds and two or three TEs; the TEs
it lets through that filter are the position's sure things.

![Net value of taking the TE over the same-pick WR/RB, by ADP round band and format](../figures/te_when.png)

Positive everywhere. A drafted TE has beaten the WR/RBs at his slot in
every band, and round 10+ is the best cell on the board.

The yardstick flatters a TE pick two ways. A dead TE pick "only" costs
the TE12 baseline (~80 pts) while a dead RB pick costs the RB30
baseline (~110). And the WR/RB gets no credit for bench value — a WR35
season still fills your flex in bye weeks; a TE9 season is a cut. The
middle bands' +11-to-+17 is well inside what those two biases can
manufacture. The R1–4 and R10+ numbers are too big for that excuse.

## 4. …and the cliff that rules out the middle

![Odds a drafted TE finishes top-6, by ADP round band](../figures/te_cliff.png)

| ADP band | n | top-3 (std) | top-6 (std) | outside top-12 (std) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1–2 | 7 | 71% | 86% | 0% |
| R3–4 | 19 | 32% | 53% | 21% |
| R5–6 | 23 | 22% | 30% | 52% |
| R7–9 | 32 | 0% | 16% | 59% |
| R10–13 | 58 | 7% | 14% | 64% |

(Half/PPR within a few points everywhere — the cliff is
format-proof.) Past round 6, TE ADP carries almost no information: a
round-7 TE and a round-12 TE are the same lottery ticket at different
prices. That is what kills the middle. The round 5–9 TE offers
round-12 odds at a round-5-to-9 price, and the modest same-pick edge
in section 3 doesn't cover walking out of the meat of the draft with a
52-to-64%-chance non-starter as your only TE. Hence the two doors:

1. **Elite door** — one of the ~2-3 consensus elite TEs, rounds 1–4.
   Historically fine-to-good on points; best of all in PPR. But see
   section 6: take it only when he *falls* to you, never by reaching.
2. **Wait door** — nothing until round 10+, ideally two darts or a
   streaming plan (finding 05). Best net value per pick; 1-in-7
   top-6 odds per dart, so the plan matters more than the name.
3. **Never the hallway between** (rounds 5–9).

Section 6 adds the rule that governs all three: **whichever door you
end up at, get there by following the board, not by deciding in
advance.** Every pre-committed version of these doors lost title rate
to a drafter who simply took the best player available.

And yes, actually draft one: the live-off-the-wire punt is drying up —
undrafted top-8 TEs went 3, 2, 3 in 2020–22, then 0, 1, 0 in 2023–25
(finding 05) — and punting your only TE to round 10 with no streaming
plan measured −1.42 all-play in the sim (finding 20).

## 5. The format dial

Every TE number above grows from std → half → PPR while the same-pick
WR/RB alternative doesn't keep pace (elite TEs are 90-catch players;
TE12 catches ~50): the all-26 net goes +19 → +25 → +34, ex-Kelce +6 →
+9 → +13, the R1–2 band +25 → +36 → +48. Rule of thumb: in full PPR,
move the elite TEs up about half a round to a round from where you'd
slot them in standard. In our standard family league the elite door is
at its least valuable — real, but a luxury, not a priority.

## 6. Does any of this win titles? (sim v5, added 2026-08-06)

Everything above is **season points**. Points are not trophies. Does
buying the early TE raise the championship rate? `te_rN` refuses a TE
before round N then buys the best one left, swept N = 2…12 against an
identical field, 3,600 seasons per arm, paired on seed (sim *i* of
year *Y* faces the same room, seat and injuries in every arm).

Read this table through the **price** column, which is the whole
story:

| arm | price paid for the TE | all-play | vs ctl | titles | vs ctl (±se) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **control** (just take the board) | −15.4 (a discount) | .5957 | — | **19.8%** | — |
| TE in round 2 | **+8.4 (reached)** | .5726 | −.023 | 16.1% | **−3.7 ± 0.9** |
| TE in round 3 | +1.4 (at par) | .5728 | −.023 | 18.1% | −1.7 ± 0.9 |
| TE in round 4 | −2.8 | .5850 | −.011 | 18.4% | −1.4 ± 0.8 |
| TE in round 5 | −4.1 | .5865 | −.009 | 20.4% | +0.6 ± 0.8 |
| TE in round 6 | −6.3 | .5901 | −.006 | 20.6% | +0.8 ± 0.8 |
| TE in round 8 | −11.8 | .5921 | −.004 | 19.4% | −0.4 ± 0.9 |
| TE in round 10 | −23.1 | .5878 | −.008 | 19.0% | −0.8 ± 0.9 |
| TE in round 12 | −19.0 | .5880 | −.008 | 18.7% | −1.1 ± 0.8 |
| TE r10 + streaming | −23.1 | .5908 | −.005 | 17.6% | **−2.2 ± 0.8** |

**No arm beat the control.** Committing in advance to *any* TE
timing — early door or wait door — lost to simply taking the board and
letting the position come to you. That is
[finding 03](03-adp-discipline-is-not-an-edge.md) again: discipline is
the edge.

**The gradient is in the price, not the position.** Title loss tracks
how far above market the arm had to buy: reach 8 picks and lose 3.7
points of title rate; pay par and lose ~1.7; take him at a discount
and the loss vanishes into noise. The control's own most-common first
TE is *Kelce* — at a 15-pick discount. The winning behavior was never
"avoid the elite TE," it was **"don't pay a premium to guarantee you
get one."**

**This does not overturn sections 1–5, and it does not confirm the
naive reading of them either.** Finding 33's +19 points was measured
on TEs who went *at their ADP in real drafts* — you can take that deal
when the board offers it. What costs you is pre-committing to the
position, because then you must pay whatever the room asks. Points and
titles come apart exactly there.

Limits:

- Any hard constraint on an ADP drafter costs something, so the sweep
  cannot prove the elite tier is *uniquely* bad — only that
  pre-commitment is.
- Paired title-rate resolution is ±1.7%. Every "unresolved" cell means
  too small to see at this n, not zero. Only the round-2 and
  wait-plus-stream losses clear that bar.
- The sim's room is the calibrated family field
  ([METHODS.md](METHODS.md)). A room that hoards TEs early would
  change the price column and could move the answer.

Reproduce: `venv/bin/python analysis/te_timing_titles.py -n 600`
(`--reuse` re-reports from the saved per-sim arrays).

| Sam LaPorta | 85 | 79 | 76 | hallway |
| Kincaid · Gadsden · Henry · Goedert · Andrews · Ferguson | 87–105 | | | hallway |
| George Kittle | 109 | 100 | 97 | **wait** |

The elite door in 2026 is exactly two players, and it is genuinely
early — McBride in round 2, Bowers in round 3, both inside the top 21
picks once receptions count. Then thirteen tight ends are priced from
pick 51 to pick 105: that entire block is the hallway this finding
says not to buy. The wait door opens around Kittle at 109.

Read through section 6: **do not plan your draft around McBride or
Bowers.** At ADP 23 and 30 in standard they are par-price buys, and
paying par measured ~1.7 points of title rate worse than just taking
the board. The version that wins is passive. If one of them is still
there when the board says he's the best player left (i.e. he has
slipped past his ADP to you), take him without hesitation. Otherwise
take the running back or receiver in front of you and let the position
come to you later. If you leave the early rounds without one, skip the
whole 51–105 block and take your dart in round 10+.

This is a snapshot of a market that moves through August. Re-check
before the draft rather than trusting these exact numbers.

(An earlier draft of this section ranked 2026 tight ends by the
projection model's predicted PPG and flagged where the model

model belongs nowhere near list order — every step from ADP toward it

## Methodology

- ADP boards `data/adp_2018..2025.json` (FFC 12-team), matched to
  gsis ids via season rosters (same matching as finding 29); every
  drafted QB/RB/WR/TE, n=1,352.
- Scoring: league-exact per-category floors (`player_model.league_pts`)
  with the reception knob at 0 / 0.5 / 1.0; weeks 1–17.
- A drafted player with no NFL season scores 0 (busts count, injuries
  count). Finishes are by season total, as in finding 29.
- "Same pick" = WR/RBs with ADP within ±6 overall picks, same draft.
- Reproduce: `venv/bin/python analysis/te_same_pick.py` (prints every
  table above and writes the four `figures/te_*.png`).

## Caveats

- 26 early-TE picks is a small sample carried by few players: Kelce 7,
  Kittle 5, Andrews 3, Ertz 2, Pitts 2, Waller 2. "The market vets
  early TEs well" rests on roughly ten distinct careers, and a
  Kelce-grade eight-year run may not exist in the next window.
- The yardstick's two pro-TE biases (bust-floor asymmetry, no bench
  credit) are argued in section 3; they cloud the middle bands, not
  the early rounds or the cliff.
- One ADP board serves all three formats; real half/PPR rooms draft
  TEs and WRs slightly earlier, which would shave the half/PPR edges.
- ±6 picks is one window; spot checks at ±4 and ±10 move means a few
  points without changing any sign.
- None of this measures the weekly start/sit convenience of an elite
  TE (never streaming, never sweating waivers) — real, but unpriced
  here.
